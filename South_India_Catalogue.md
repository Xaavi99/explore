## WELCOME TO SOUTH INDIA

### About South India

South India is a place where three thousand years of trade, faith and craft still shape everyday life. It is a region of palm-lined waterways and cloud-covered tea hills, of temple towns where worship has continued unbroken for a millennium, and of coastlines that carried spices to Rome, Arabia and Europe long before modern maps existed. Visitors often arrive expecting beaches and leave remembering conversations, with a boatman, a plantation family, a temple guide whose grandfather held the same role.

**FORT KOCHI & CHINESE FISHING NETS**

Fort Kochi is where the modern history of India's spice trade begins. Portuguese, Dutch and British powers each governed this small peninsula in turn, and each left something behind: a churchyard, a warehouse façade, a street name. Today the quarter is best experienced slowly, on foot, where colonial bungalows sit alongside working fishing families.

Along the northern shore stand the cantilevered fishing nets, introduced through contact with Chinese traders centuries ago and still operated by teams of men pulling counterweighted ropes by hand. At sunset the silhouettes of the nets against the harbour are among the most photographed scenes in India, and the men working them are usually happy to explain the mechanism.

---

**MATTANCHERRY PALACE**

Built by the Portuguese and presented to the Raja of Kochi in the mid-sixteenth century, Mattancherry Palace, known locally as the Dutch Palace after later renovations, is modest from the outside and extraordinary within.

Its central chambers hold some of the finest Hindu temple murals in India, covering the walls floor to ceiling with scenes from the Ramayana and Puranic legend in mineral pigments that remain vivid after four centuries. The coronation hall displays the palanquins, weapons and ceremonial dress of the Kochi royal family. It is a small museum, easily seen in an hour, and one of the most rewarding stops in the city.

---

**PARADESI SYNAGOGUE & JEW TOWN**

Constructed in 1568, the Paradesi Synagogue is the oldest active synagogue in the Commonwealth and the enduring monument of Kerala's Jewish community, whose presence on this coast dates back nearly two thousand years.

The interior is floored with hand-painted blue-and-white Chinese tiles, no two identical, and lit by Belgian glass chandeliers. Copper plates recording the rights granted to the community by a Hindu ruler are held here. The surrounding lanes of Jew Town are now lined with antique dealers, spice merchants and small cafés, making it one of the most atmospheric quarters in Kochi for an unhurried walk.

---

**ST. FRANCIS CHURCH**

Built in 1503, St. Francis Church is the oldest European church in India and a quiet, powerful marker of the age of exploration. Vasco da Gama died in Kochi in 1524 and was buried here; although his remains were later moved to Lisbon, his original gravestone remains in the floor of the nave.

The building itself is plain, whitewashed and cool, with punkah fans still suspended from the ceiling beams. It takes only a few minutes to visit, but it anchors the whole story of why this small harbour mattered so much to the world.

---

**KATHAKALI PERFORMANCE**

Kathakali is Kerala's classical dance-drama, and one of the most visually extraordinary performance traditions anywhere: elaborately painted faces, towering headdresses, and a vocabulary of eye and hand movements capable of narrating an entire epic without a spoken word.

The best introduction includes arriving early to watch the performers apply their makeup, a process taking well over an hour, during which the transformation from person to character happens in front of you. A short guided explanation of the mudras beforehand makes the performance itself far more legible to a first-time viewer.

---

**KALARIPAYATTU DEMONSTRATION**

Kalaripayattu is among the oldest martial arts in the world, developed in Kerala and traditionally practised in a sunken earthen pit called a *kalari*. Training combines strikes, grappling, weapons work and an acrobatic agility that is genuinely startling to watch at close quarters.

Demonstrations include staff and sword sequences, high leaping attacks, and the flexibility exercises that begin every practitioner's training. The art is also connected to a traditional system of massage and bone-setting therapy, still practised, which forms part of Kerala's wider healing tradition.

---

**MARINE DRIVE**

Marine Drive is Kochi's waterfront promenade, a pedestrian walkway curving along the backwater channel facing the harbour. It is where the city comes to walk in the evening: families, students, vendors selling roasted corn and tender coconut.

The Rainbow Bridge and Chinese Fishing Net Bridge offer clear views across to Vypin and Bolgatty islands, and the light in the hour before sunset is exceptional. Short harbour ferries depart from nearby jetties for those who want to see the working port from the water.

---

**SPICE MARKETS OF MATTANCHERRY**

The lanes behind Jew Town remain a functioning wholesale spice district, and the sensory experience is the point: sacks of black pepper, cardamom, turmeric, ginger and star anise open to the street, with the smell carrying a block in either direction.

These are working warehouses rather than tourist shops, and a guided visit gives context that a self-guided wander cannot: how pepper is graded, why Kerala's cardamom commands the price it does, and how this small district was once a node in a trade network reaching Venice and Alexandria.

---

**FOLKLORE MUSEUM**

The Kerala Folklore Museum is a private collection housed in a building assembled from salvaged elements of old Kerala homes and temples, carved wooden pillars, gabled roofs and joinery reconstructed across three floors.

Inside are more than four thousand artefacts spanning masks, ritual objects, musical instruments, sculpture and textiles from across Kerala's regions. The top floor holds a wooden theatre where evening performances are sometimes staged. For visitors interested in the material culture behind the performances they will see elsewhere, it is the single best stop in the city.

---

**CHERAI BEACH**

Cherai lies on Vypin Island, around forty minutes from Fort Kochi, and offers something unusual, a long stretch of calm golden sand with backwater canals running directly behind it, so sea and lagoon sit within a few hundred metres of each other.

The beach is broad, shallow and rarely crowded on weekdays. Small Chinese fishing nets operate along the backwater side, and the drive out passes through coconut groves and village settlements. It works well as a half-day addition to a Kochi itinerary, particularly for travellers wanting a slower afternoon.

---

**BACKWATER VILLAGE CANOE RIDE**

Away from the large houseboats, a country canoe ride through the narrow canals around Kochi offers the quieter, closer version of the backwaters. The boat is small, paddled or poled rather than engine-driven, and passes directly through village life: women washing at the water's edge, coir being twisted by hand, children crossing on footbridges.

Routes around Kumbalangi and the islands south of the city pass Chinese nets, prawn farms and toddy-tapping palms. Two to three hours is usually enough, and it is often the experience guests remember most clearly.

**TEA PLANTATIONS & ESTATE WALK**

Munnar's tea gardens were planted by British and Scottish planters from the 1880s onward, and the landscape they created, hillsides contoured into an unbroken green corduroy, is now the defining image of highland Kerala.

A guided estate walk takes you into the working plantation rather than past it: how the bushes are pruned to table height, why only two leaves and a bud are taken, and how the pluckers judge by hand what a machine still cannot. Many estates finish the walk with a tasting of grades that never reach export markets.

---

**ERAVIKULAM NATIONAL PARK**

Established to protect the Nilgiri tahr, a mountain goat found nowhere else, Eravikulam is the most reliable place in the world to see the species. The animals are habituated to the park's shuttle route and often graze within a few metres of visitors.

The park protects high-altitude shola grassland, a rare and fragile ecosystem, and is also the site of the *Neelakurinji* bloom, a purple-blue flowering that occurs only once every twelve years and carpets entire hillsides when it does. Access is by park shuttle bus from Rajamalai.

---

**TEA MUSEUM**

The Tata Tea Museum at Nallathanni sets out the full story of how tea came to these hills: the clearing of forest, the building of the ropeways and mono-rails that moved leaf down the mountains, and the machinery that processed it.

Original equipment is displayed alongside photographs and estate records, and a working demonstration takes visitors through withering, rolling, oxidation and drying. It is a genuinely informative hour and makes the plantation walk that follows considerably more interesting.

---

**MATTUPETTY DAM**

Built as part of a hydroelectric scheme, the Mattupetty reservoir now sits in a landscape of grassland and eucalyptus roughly thirteen kilometres from Munnar town, with speedboat and pedal boat hire on the water.

Nearby is the Indo-Swiss dairy project, where high-yield cattle breeds are raised, and the surrounding shola forest is good for birdwatching. The drive up passes some of the most photographed stretches of the Munnar road, and the reservoir itself is at its best in early morning before the mist lifts.

---

**TOP STATION VIEWPOINT**

At around 1,700 metres, Top Station is the highest point on the Munnar road and marks the boundary with Tamil Nadu. It takes its name from the aerial ropeway that once carried tea down to the valley below.

On a clear morning the view extends across the Theni plains far below, with cloud typically filling the valley and burning off as the sun rises. The site is also one of the locations where the Neelakurinji blooms. Arriving early is essential, by mid-morning the viewpoint is often entirely in cloud.

---

**ANAMUDI PEAK**

Anamudi is the highest mountain in India south of the Himalaya, rising to 2,695 metres within Eravikulam National Park. Its name means "elephant's forehead," describing the shape of the summit dome.

The peak dominates the skyline from much of the park, and the surrounding high grassland supports tahr, gaur and occasionally leopard. Summit access is restricted to protect the ecosystem, but the views of the massif from within the park are among the finest mountain landscapes in South India.

---

**ATTUKAD WATERFALLS**

Set between Munnar and Pallivasal, Attukad falls through a wooded gorge in a broad curtain, reached by a short walk and a flight of steps from the road.

The falls are at their most powerful just after the monsoon, between September and January, when the flow is heavy and the surrounding forest at its greenest. The approach passes through cardamom and coffee smallholdings, and the site is considerably quieter than the roadside falls closer to town.

---

**KUNDALA LAKE**

Kundala is an artificial lake surrounded by tea gardens and forest, sitting on the road between Munnar and Top Station. Shikara-style boats and pedal boats operate on the water.

The lake is best known for its setting, the cherry blossom trees that flower along its banks in spring, and the reflection of the surrounding hills in still morning water. It is a natural stop when travelling to Top Station and pairs well with an early departure.

---

**SPICE PLANTATION VISIT**

The slopes around Munnar and neighbouring Thekkady grow cardamom, pepper, nutmeg, clove and cinnamon, often intercropped in small family holdings under forest shade.

A guided plantation walk shows how each crop grows and is harvested: pepper vines climbing living trees, cardamom growing at ground level in deep shade, nutmeg splitting to reveal its scarlet mace. Most visits end with tasting and the chance to buy directly from the growers at a fraction of retail prices.

---

**SUNRISE TREK**

Munnar's high ridges are at their most striking in the first hour of daylight, when cloud sits in the valleys below and the tea gardens emerge slowly out of mist.

Guided sunrise treks range from short walks to viewpoints near town to longer routes up ridge lines requiring a pre-dawn start. Routes are chosen to match fitness and conditions, and the descent typically finishes in time for breakfast at the estate. A naturalist guide adds considerably to the walk, as the shola forest edges are rich in endemic birdlife.

**OVERNIGHT HOUSEBOAT CRUISE**

The Kerala houseboat, or *kettuvallam*, began life as a rice barge: hulls built from jackwood planks stitched together with coir rope, without a single nail, and roofed in woven palm. Converted for guests, they now offer the single most distinctive way to experience the backwaters.

A private houseboat carries its own crew and cook. Meals are prepared onboard through the day, often fish bought that morning from a passing canoe. The boat moves slowly through the main channels, moors at a village bank before dusk, and the evening is spent watching the water go quiet.

---

**VEMBANAD LAKE**

Vembanad is the longest lake in India, stretching some ninety-six kilometres and forming the heart of the Kerala backwater system. Its waters are separated from the sea by a barrage that keeps salt out of the surrounding farmland.

The lake supports fishing communities, duck farming and Chinese-style dip nets, and its eastern shore holds the bird sanctuary at Kumarakom. Crossing the open water, particularly in the light of late afternoon, gives a sense of scale that the narrow canals cannot.

---

**COIR VILLAGE & WORKSHOP**

Alappuzha was, for more than a century, the centre of the world's coir industry: the coconut-fibre trade that gave the town its docks, its warehouses and its old nickname, the Venice of the East.

In the villages along the canals, coir is still retted, beaten and spun by hand and on treadle-driven wheels. A workshop visit shows the full process from husk to yarn to woven mat, and the artisans are generally glad to demonstrate. It is a rare surviving example of a craft that shaped an entire district's economy.

---

**ALAPPUZHA BEACH & PIER**

Alappuzha's beach is marked by a cast-iron pier built in 1862, now partly ruined and standing out into the surf, and by a lighthouse dating from the same era of the town's commercial peak.

It is a working, local beach rather than a resort one, busiest in the evening when families gather and vendors set up along the sand. The pier is a striking piece of industrial archaeology and a reminder that this quiet town was once one of the busiest ports on the Malabar coast.

---

**SNAKE BOAT RACE** *(seasonal)*

The *vallam kali* snake boat races are among the great spectacles of South India, narrow chundan vallam hulls over thirty metres long, each crewed by more than a hundred rowers moving in unison to the rhythm of onboard singers.

The Nehru Trophy Boat Race on Punnamada Lake, held in August, is the most famous, drawing enormous crowds. Smaller races run through the same season at Champakulam, Aranmula and elsewhere. Visits must be timed precisely and booked well ahead; when the timing aligns, nothing else in the region compares.

---

**VILLAGE CANOE CRUISE**

Where the houseboat travels the main channels, the country canoe goes where the houseboat cannot, into narrow canals barely wider than the boat itself, threading between garden plots and homes.

Poled or paddled by a local boatman, the canoe passes lock gates, footbridges and small temples, moving at walking pace and near silence. Two to three hours is typical. For travellers who find the houseboats too large or too visible, this is the more intimate alternative, and often the more memorable one.

---

**PADDY FIELDS OF KUTTANAD**

Kuttanad is one of the very few places on earth where farming is carried out consistently below sea level, in some places more than two metres below it, on land reclaimed from the lake and protected by earthen bunds and continuous pumping.

The result is a landscape unlike anywhere else in India: green paddy stretching to the horizon, cut by raised bunds and canals along which boats pass at a level above the fields. It is best seen between September and November, when the crop is high and the light is clear.

---

**TODDY SHOP EXPERIENCE**

The *kallu shappu*, or toddy shop, is a Kerala institution, a simple thatched or tiled roadside room serving fresh toddy tapped from coconut palms, alongside some of the most robustly spiced food in the state.

Toddy is drawn at dawn, mildly sweet at first and fermenting through the day. It is served with dishes built to match it: tapioca with fish curry, fried karimeen, duck roast, beef ularthiyathu. A guided visit to a well-chosen shop is an authentic and genuinely convivial experience.

---

**MARARI BEACH**

Marari, a short drive north of Alappuzha, is a working fishing village that has become one of Kerala's most relaxed stretches of coast: long, wide, and lined with coconut palms rather than development.

Fishermen still launch and land catamarans directly from the sand each morning, and the village behind the beach is unhurried. Marari is generally used as the final, slow stop at the end of a Kerala itinerary, after the hills and the backwaters, and is well served by a small number of high-quality properties.

**KUMARAKOM BIRD SANCTUARY**

Set on the eastern shore of Vembanad Lake across fourteen acres of former rubber plantation and mangrove, the Kumarakom Bird Sanctuary is one of the finest birding sites in South India.

Resident species include darter, egret, heron, cormorant and the Indian tree pie, while the winter months bring migratory arrivals: teal, lapwing, and pintail among them, some travelling from Siberia. The sanctuary is walked along raised paths and boardwalks, and an early start with a naturalist guide makes an enormous difference to what is seen.

---

**VEMBANAD BACKWATERS**

At Kumarakom the backwaters open into their broadest expanse, where Vembanad Lake widens toward the horizon and the character shifts from enclosed canal to open water.

Cruises range from short shikara trips into the mangrove channels to longer crossings toward Alappuzha. Chinese dip nets stand along the shoreline, and the lake supports both clam collection and duck farming, flocks of several hundred birds herded across the water by a single boatman.

---

**SUNSET CRUISE**

A private sunset cruise from Kumarakom crosses open water at the hour when the lake is at its stillest and the light at its warmest, with the far shore reducing to a low dark line and the sky reflecting whole.

Boats are typically small shikara-style craft with open sides and a canopy. Tea and snacks are usually served onboard. It is a simple experience, lasting under two hours, and is consistently one of the highest-rated inclusions on any Kerala itinerary.

---

**AYURVEDA & WELLNESS TREATMENT**

Kerala is the home of Ayurveda, and the treatments offered here descend from a continuous scholarly and practical tradition rather than a spa concept imported later.

A proper course begins with consultation by a qualified physician, who determines constitution and prescribes accordingly. Treatments range from single sessions, *abhyanga* oil massage, *shirodhara* oil-pouring, to full *panchakarma* detoxification programmes lasting one to three weeks. Choosing a genuinely accredited centre matters greatly, and is something we handle directly.

---

**VILLAGE & ARTISAN WALK**

The villages behind Kumarakom's waterfront support crafts that have adapted to the landscape, coir spun from coconut husk, and screwpine leaf woven into mats and baskets by women working from home.

A guided walk moves through garden plots, past small temples and toddy palms, and into workshops where the crafts are demonstrated first-hand. Several local properties run structured artisan programmes with village cooperatives, ensuring the visit supports the makers directly.

---

**KAYAKING THE CANALS**

Kayaking gives access to the narrowest waterways at the quietest possible pace, no motor, no crew, and a seat close enough to the water to hear it.

Guided paddles usually set out at first light, when the canals are busiest with local traffic and the birdlife is most active. Routes are gentle and suitable for beginners, threading between garden plots and under low footbridges. It is the most active way to experience the backwaters and appeals particularly to travellers who find the houseboats too passive.

---

**PATHIRAMANAL ISLAND**

Pathiramanal is a small uninhabited island in the middle of Vembanad Lake, reachable only by boat, whose name translates roughly as "sands of midnight."

The island is protected and holds dense growth that attracts a considerable range of resident and migratory birds, making it a natural extension of a Kumarakom birding itinerary. The crossing itself is part of the appeal, open lake in both directions, with the mainland reduced to a distant fringe of palm.

**PERIYAR TIGER RESERVE**

Periyar protects nearly 925 square kilometres of the Western Ghats, one of the world's recognised biodiversity hotspots, and is home to tiger, elephant, gaur, sambar, Nilgiri langur and more than 260 bird species.

What distinguishes Periyar is its community model: former poachers were retrained as guides and protection staff, and now lead the reserve's walking and rafting programmes. Their tracking knowledge is exceptional, and the arrangement has become a widely studied example of conservation that works with local livelihoods rather than against them.

---

**PERIYAR LAKE BOAT SAFARI**

The lake at the centre of the reserve was created in 1895 when the Mullaperiyar Dam flooded the valley, leaving the bleached trunks of drowned trees still standing above the waterline, a landscape that is strange and quietly beautiful.

Boats cruise the shoreline where elephant, gaur and sambar come down to drink, particularly in the dry months when other water sources recede. Early morning departures offer the best light and the highest chance of sightings. Wildlife viewing is never guaranteed, but the setting alone justifies the trip.

---

**SPICE PLANTATION TOUR**

The hills around Thekkady are among India's principal cardamom-growing districts, and the plantations here typically grow several spices together under shade: pepper vines on standing trees, cardamom beneath, nutmeg and clove interspersed.

A guided walk explains the growing cycle, the harvesting methods and the grading that determines price. Visitors crush leaves, split pods and taste as they go. Most estates conclude with a tasting and direct sale, and the quality available at source is markedly better than what is generally exported.

---

**BAMBOO RAFTING**

Run by the reserve's own guide teams, the bamboo rafting programme combines a long day's trekking through the forest with several hours drifting on the lake aboard a raft of lashed bamboo.

Groups are small and accompanied by armed forest guards and naturalists. The route moves through grassland and forest edge where wildlife is regularly encountered on foot, and the silence of the raft allows close approach on the water. It is the most immersive way to experience Periyar and requires reasonable fitness.

---

**BORDER HIKING TRAIL**

The border hike follows patrol routes along the reserve's boundary with Tamil Nadu, through forest, grassland and stream crossings, led by reserve guides who once knew these paths as poachers.

The walk is moderate rather than demanding and lasts several hours. It offers the best chance of encountering wildlife on foot, gaur, sambar and elephant are all regularly seen, and the guides' knowledge of tracks, alarm calls and feeding signs turns the walk into a lesson in reading forest.

---

**ELEPHANT INTERACTION**

Elephants are central to Kerala's cultural and natural life, and Thekkady offers opportunities to see them both wild within the reserve and in managed care outside it.

We work only with centres meeting genuine welfare standards, no riding, no performance, and interaction limited to feeding, bathing and observation under keeper supervision. Guests who prefer to see elephants only in the wild are always encouraged to do so, and the reserve's boat safari and walking programmes offer good chances of that.

---

**KALARIPAYATTU & KATHAKALI EVENING**

Thekkady's evening programmes offer both of Kerala's great performance traditions in a single sitting, the martial art of Kalaripayattu and the classical dance-drama of Kathakali.

Venues here are small and the seating close, which suits both forms: the Kalari demonstrations are genuinely startling at short range, and the Kathakali makeup and mudra explanation preceding the performance makes the storytelling accessible. It is a natural way to fill an evening after a day in the reserve.

---

**EDAKKAL CAVES**

The Edakkal Caves are not caves in the true sense but a cleft formed where a boulder fell across a fissure in the rock, creating a chamber high on Ambukuthi Hill.

Their significance lies in the walls: petroglyphs carved into the stone, the oldest dating back more than six thousand years, depicting human figures, animals, wheels and symbols. Some carvings show scripts that have never been fully deciphered. Reaching them requires a steep climb of around forty-five minutes, and the view from the entrance is exceptional.

---

**CHEMBRA PEAK TREK**

Chembra is the highest peak in Wayanad at just over 2,100 metres, and its trail is the best-known trek in the district. The path climbs through grassland and shola forest with the valley opening out behind at every turn.

Partway up lies a small permanent lake, naturally heart-shaped, which has become the trek's signature image. Permits are issued in limited numbers from the forest office at the base, and an early start is strongly advised, cloud typically closes the summit by late morning.

---

**BANASURA SAGAR DAM**

Banasura Sagar is the largest earth dam in India and the second largest in Asia, built from packed stone and earth across a valley below Banasura Hill.

When the reservoir fills, the tops of submerged hills emerge as a scattering of small islands, and speedboat trips run out among them. The setting, dam wall, still water, forested ridges behind, is striking, and the surrounding area offers good short walks and viewpoints for those wanting to stay longer.

---

**SOOCHIPARA WATERFALLS**

Also known as Sentinel Rock Falls, Soochipara drops in three tiers through dense forest, with a pool at the base deep enough for swimming and cliff faces used by rock climbers.

The approach is a walk of around two kilometres through forest, descending steadily. The falls are at their most powerful from June through January. Spray reaches well beyond the pool, and the surrounding tree cover keeps the site cool even in the middle of the day.

---

**WAYANAD WILDLIFE SANCTUARY**

Part of the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, the Wayanad sanctuary connects directly with Bandipur and Nagarhole across the state borders, forming one of the most important contiguous elephant habitats in southern India.

Safaris run through Muthanga and Tholpetty ranges in the early morning and late afternoon. Elephant, gaur, sambar, spotted deer and langur are seen regularly; tiger and leopard are present but rarely encountered. The forest here is drier and more open than the Ghats to the west, which makes viewing easier.

---

**THIRUNELLI TEMPLE**

Set at over 900 metres among forested hills, Thirunelli is one of Kerala's most remote and atmospheric temples, dedicated to Vishnu and believed to be more than a thousand years old.

A stream called the Papanasini runs beside it, and pilgrims come to perform ancestral rites in its waters. The temple's stone construction, its thirty granite pillars, and its setting entirely surrounded by forest give it a stillness that the larger and busier temples of the plains cannot offer.

---

**COFFEE & SPICE ESTATES**

Wayanad's plateau is coffee country, and the estates here typically grow robusta and arabica under shade trees, with pepper vines climbing the same trunks and cardamom in the understorey.

A guided estate walk covers the growing and processing cycle, picking, pulping, fermenting, drying, and finishes with cupping. Many estates are family-run and have been for generations, and the conversation with the growers is often as valuable as the walk itself.

---

**POOKODE LAKE**

Pookode is a small natural freshwater lake set in evergreen forest, shaped roughly like a map of India and surrounded by a walking path of about two kilometres.

Pedal boats operate on the water, and a small freshwater aquarium beside the lake displays local fish species. It is a gentle, easily accessible stop, well suited to travellers who want a short walk in forest without a demanding trek, and it works naturally into the drive between Kalpetta and Vythiri.

**VARKALA CLIFF & PAPANASAM BEACH**

Varkala's cliff is a geological rarity: a wall of red laterite rising directly above the Arabian Sea, the only such formation along the Kerala coast, and designated a geological monument.

Along its top runs a path lined with cafés, shops and small guesthouses; below lies Papanasam Beach, whose name means "destroyer of sins." Mineral springs emerge from the cliff face and run down onto the sand, and pilgrims come to bathe here as an act of purification. The sunset view from the cliff path is among the finest in South India.

---

**JANARDANASWAMY TEMPLE**

Overlooking Papanasam Beach, the Janardanaswamy Temple is more than two thousand years old by tradition and is one of Kerala's important Vishnu shrines, sometimes called the Benares of the South.

Its bell is said to have come from a Dutch ship whose captain donated it in gratitude for surviving a storm. The temple is closely tied to the beach below, where ancestral rites are performed, and the combination of active worship, antiquity and sea setting makes it a compelling short visit.

---

**SIVAGIRI MUTT**

Sivagiri is the headquarters of the movement founded by Sree Narayana Guru, the social reformer whose campaigns against caste discrimination reshaped Kerala society in the early twentieth century.

The complex holds his samadhi and a temple, and draws large numbers of pilgrims, particularly during the December–January Sivagiri pilgrimage. For visitors interested in why Kerala's social indicators differ so markedly from much of India, this is the most direct place to encounter that history.

---

**KAPPIL LAKE & BACKWATER**

A short drive north of Varkala, Kappil is the point where backwater and sea run within a few dozen metres of each other, separated by a narrow strip of land and joined by a small estuary.

Boats can be hired on the lake side, and a bridge crossing gives a clear view of both waters at once. It is far quieter than the main backwater centres and works well as a half-day trip combining a short cruise with an uncrowded beach.

---

**AYURVEDA & YOGA**

Varkala has become one of Kerala's principal centres for yoga and Ayurvedic treatment, with the cliff-top setting drawing practitioners and long-stay visitors from across the world.

Programmes range from single treatments and drop-in classes to structured multi-week retreats combining daily practice, consultation and prescribed therapy. As elsewhere in Kerala, quality varies considerably; we work only with centres holding proper accreditation and qualified physicians on staff.

---

**ANJENGO FORT**

Built by the East India Company in 1696, Anjengo, Anchuthengu, was the first English settlement on the Malabar coast and a critical early foothold in their expansion into India.

The fort's walls, cemetery and lighthouse remain, standing between the sea and the backwater. It is quiet and little visited, but for anyone interested in how European trading power took hold on this coastline, it is a more revealing site than its modest scale suggests.

---

**PARAGLIDING & SURFING**

The cliff and the prevailing sea breeze make Varkala one of the few paragliding sites on the Kerala coast, with tandem flights launching from the cliff top and running along the shoreline.

The beach also has a small but established surf scene, with schools offering lessons and board hire for beginners. Conditions are best outside the monsoon months. Both activities are weather-dependent and are arranged with operators holding proper certification and insurance.

Padmanabhaswamy Temple admits Hindus only, with a strict dress code; the exterior and surrounding complex remain worth visiting regardless. Kovalam's sea has strong currents in places, swim only in flagged areas.

**LIGHTHOUSE BEACH**

The southernmost and best-known of Kovalam's crescents takes its name from the red-and-white striped Vizhinjam Lighthouse standing on the headland above it.

The lighthouse can be climbed, and the view from the gallery takes in the whole sweep of the coast: the beaches curving north, fishing boats drawn up on the sand, and the Western Ghats visible inland on clear days. The beach itself is the liveliest in Kovalam, lined with cafés and shops along its promenade.

---

**HAWA BEACH & SAMUDRA BEACH**

North of the lighthouse, Hawa Beach, historically called Eve's Beach, and Samudra Beach continue the sequence of crescents, each progressively quieter than the last.

Hawa is where the local fishing fleet works, and the morning catch is landed and sorted directly on the sand. Samudra, further north, is the most peaceful of the three, backed by a handful of resorts and rarely busy. Together they allow a visitor to choose the atmosphere they want within a fifteen-minute walk.

---

**VIZHINJAM ROCK-CUT CAVE TEMPLE**

Dating to the eighth century, this small rock-cut shrine near Vizhinjam harbour is one of the oldest surviving pieces of stone architecture in the region, carved from a single granite outcrop.

Its unfinished sculptures of Shiva and Parvati remain in place, and the shrine's simplicity, a single chamber cut into rock, contrasts sharply with the elaborate temple architecture that developed later. It sits within a working fishing harbour and takes only a short visit, but it is genuinely ancient.

---

**PADMANABHASWAMY TEMPLE, TRIVANDRUM**

Sree Padmanabhaswamy Temple is among the most remarkable religious buildings in India: a Dravidian-style complex where the presiding deity, Vishnu, reclines on the serpent Anantha in an image visible only through three separate doors.

The temple gained worldwide attention when vaults beneath it were opened, revealing accumulated treasure of extraordinary value, most of it untouched for centuries. Entry is restricted to Hindus and subject to a strict dress code, but the gopuram, tank and surrounding fort quarter can be explored by anyone.

---

**NAPIER MUSEUM & ART GALLERY**

Built in the 1880s in an Indo-Saracenic style that draws on Kerala, Chinese, Mughal and Italian influences, the Napier Museum is as much an architectural exhibit as a container for one.

Its collection includes bronzes, temple carriages, ivory work and ceremonial ornaments, while the adjoining Sri Chitra Art Gallery holds an important collection of Raja Ravi Varma paintings alongside Mughal and Tanjore works. The building's naturally ventilated design keeps it cool without air conditioning.

---

**POOVAR ISLAND & ESTUARY**

South of Kovalam, Poovar is the point where the Neyyar River, the backwaters and the Arabian Sea converge, separated by a sandbar reachable only by boat.

The crossing passes through mangrove channels before opening onto a golden beach with sea on one side and still water on the other. A small number of floating and waterfront properties operate here. It is quiet, self-contained and among the most photogenic settings on the southern Kerala coast.

---

**AYURVEDIC RETREATS**

The Kovalam and Poovar coast holds some of Kerala's longest-established Ayurvedic retreat properties, several of them purpose-built around treatment rather than adapted from resorts.

Programmes typically run from one to three weeks and combine physician consultation, prescribed therapy, diet and yoga. The sea setting suits the rest that a full course requires. As always, accreditation is the deciding factor, and we place guests only with centres that meet it.

**ATHIRAPPILLY FALLS**

Athirappilly is the largest waterfall in Kerala, where the Chalakudy River drops some eighty feet in a broad curtain across a rock face at the entrance to the Sholayar forest range.

A path descends through rainforest to the base, where the scale of the fall and the volume of spray are best appreciated. The surrounding forest is one of the few places where all four South Indian hornbill species occur, including the Great Hornbill. Often called the Niagara of India, it is Kerala's single most photographed natural site.

---

**VAZHACHAL FALLS**

Five kilometres upstream from Athirappilly, Vazhachal is a broad cascade running over stepped rock, close to the road and framed by dense forest on both banks.

It is gentler than Athirappilly and easier to approach, with viewing points a short walk from the parking area. The forest here forms part of the same riparian system that supports the hornbill population, and the two falls are almost always visited together as part of a single route.

---

**CHARPA FALLS**

Charpa is the smallest of the three, falling directly beside the road so that spray reaches passing vehicles during heavy flow, a brief and memorable stop rather than a destination.

It runs strongly during and immediately after the monsoon and reduces considerably in the dry months. Its position on the route between Athirappilly and Vazhachal makes it a natural pause, and the roadside setting gives an unusually close view of the water.

---

**SHOLAYAR FOREST DRIVE**

The road beyond Vazhachal climbs into the Sholayar ranges, among the densest and least disturbed rainforest in Kerala, connecting eventually toward Valparai in Tamil Nadu.

The drive passes through tall evergreen forest with occasional openings over the valley, and wildlife, lion-tailed macaque, hornbill, elephant, is regularly encountered from the vehicle. Permits and timings are regulated. It is one of the finest forest drives in South India and rewards an early start.

---

**THUMBOORMUZHI DAM & BUTTERFLY GARDEN**

Below the falls on the Chalakudy River, Thumboormuzhi combines a small dam with a hanging bridge and a dedicated butterfly conservation garden.

The garden was among the first of its kind in Kerala and is planted specifically to support breeding populations, with well over a hundred species recorded. The site is gentle and well suited to families, and it works naturally as the final stop on a day route returning toward Kochi.

**THEYYAM RITUAL PERFORMANCE**

Theyyam is the defining cultural tradition of North Malabar and among the most extraordinary living rituals anywhere in the world. Performers of specific communities undergo hours of preparation: elaborate face painting, towering headdresses, costumes of palm and cloth, before entering a state in which they are believed to embody the deity itself.

Once transformed, the *theyyam* speaks, blesses and receives petitions from villagers, sometimes dancing through fire. It is held at village shrines rather than theatres, mainly between December and April. Witnessing one requires local knowledge and respect, and is unforgettable.

---

**BEKAL FORT**

Bekal is the largest and best-preserved fort in Kerala, covering some forty acres on a headland projecting into the Arabian Sea, its laterite walls rising directly from the water.

Built in the seventeenth century, it retains its observation tower, magazine and a tunnel running toward the shore. Unusually for a fort of this scale, it holds no palace; its purpose was purely defensive. The views from the ramparts, with sea on three sides, are exceptional, particularly in the late afternoon.

---

**ST. ANGELO'S FORT**

Built by the Portuguese in 1505 on a headland at Kannur, St. Angelo's passed to the Dutch and then the British, each leaving alterations in its laterite walls.

The fort looks out over the Mappila Bay fishing harbour, and from its bastions the whole working life of the harbour is visible below. It is compact, easily walked in an hour, and less visited than its historical significance would suggest, this was one of the earliest European fortifications in India.

---

**MUZHAPPILANGAD DRIVE-IN BEACH**

Muzhappilangad is the longest drive-in beach in Asia, a four-kilometre stretch of firm, flat sand where vehicles are permitted to drive directly along the shoreline.

Black rocks at either end protect the bay, keeping the water shallow and generally calm. The beach draws a lively local crowd, particularly at weekends, and paragliding and microlight operations run seasonally. It is one of the more genuinely unusual experiences on the Kerala coast.

---

**HANDLOOM & WEAVING COOPERATIVES**

Kannur has been a centre of handloom weaving for generations, and its cooperative societies still produce furnishing fabric and the distinctive Kerala *kasavu* cloth, off-white cotton with a gold border, on pit and frame looms.

A workshop visit shows warping, dyeing and weaving in progress, with the rhythm of a working shed rather than a demonstration. Purchases go directly to the cooperative. For visitors interested in the textile that appears at every Kerala festival and wedding, this is where it is made.

---

**VALIYAPARAMBA BACKWATERS**

Valiyaparamba is the quietest major backwater stretch in Kerala, a wide waterway fed by four rivers and scattered with small islands, running behind a long barrier of coconut palm.

Because it lies far north of the established tourist circuit, houseboat and cruise traffic here is a fraction of that at Alappuzha. The water is broad and open, the villages along it largely unaffected by tourism, and the experience is closer to what the southern backwaters offered decades ago.

---

**PAYYAMBALAM BEACH**

Payyambalam is Kannur's principal beach: a long, clean stretch of sand backed by a landscaped park and a well-known sculpture, and the place the town gathers in the evening.

It is a local beach rather than a resort one, with almost no commercial development along the sand itself. Sunset here is the main event, and the combination of a broad open shoreline and very few visitors from outside the district makes it a genuinely restful stop.

**FONTAINHAS LATIN QUARTER, PANJIM**

Fontainhas is the finest surviving Portuguese-era residential quarter in India: narrow lanes of townhouses painted in ochre, blue, green and red, with overhanging balconies, shell-inlaid windows and azulejo tiles bearing street names in Portuguese.

The convention of repainting after each monsoon has kept the colour intact for generations. The quarter is small enough to walk in an hour and rewards wandering without a route, past small chapels, bakeries and the Church of St. Sebastian with its striking crucifix.

---

**BAGA, CALANGUTE & CANDOLIM BEACHES**

The northern beach belt runs almost continuously from Candolim through Calangute to Baga, forming the busiest and most developed stretch of coastline in Goa.

Calangute is the largest and most commercial; Baga is the centre of the nightlife scene and water sports; Candolim is marginally quieter and better suited to those wanting sea without the crowds. Shacks line the sand through the season, and the beaches are best experienced early in the morning before the day fills.

---

**ANJUNA FLEA MARKET**

Started in the 1970s by travellers selling what they carried, the Anjuna flea market has grown into a sprawling weekly institution: hundreds of stalls covering textiles, silver, spices, instruments and antiques.

It runs on Wednesdays during the season and draws vendors from across India, with Tibetan, Rajasthani, Kashmiri and Gujarati traders alongside Goan ones. Bargaining is expected. Going early avoids both crowd and heat, and a guide helps considerably in judging quality and price.

---

**CHAPORA FORT**

Built by the Portuguese in 1617 on a headland above the Chapora River, the fort is now largely ruined, though its walls remain walkable and its position is extraordinary.

From the ramparts the view runs south over Vagator beach and north across the river mouth, taking in one of the best sunset panoramas in Goa. The climb from the road takes about fifteen minutes. The fort gained wide fame in India through film, and remains busiest in the hour before dusk.

---

**AGUADA FORT & LIGHTHOUSE**

Aguada was built in 1612 to defend the mouth of the Mandovi against Dutch and Maratha attack, and it succeeded, it was never taken. Its name comes from the freshwater spring within the walls that resupplied passing ships.

The complex includes a four-storey lighthouse, among the oldest of its kind in Asia, and a vast cistern beneath the courtyard. The bastions look out over the river mouth and the sea beyond, and the scale of the fortification gives a clear sense of how much this harbour was worth defending.

---

**MANDOVI RIVER CRUISE**

Evening cruises depart from the Panjim jetty and run along the Mandovi past the waterfront, the old customs house and the floating casinos, with folk music and Goan dance performed onboard.

The cruise lasts around an hour and coincides with sunset. Private charters are available for those preferring a quieter experience, and dolphin-watching trips from the river mouth operate in the morning. It is a relaxed way to see Panjim from the water.

---

**FENI DISTILLERY VISIT**

Feni is Goa's own spirit and holds Geographical Indication protection, distilled either from cashew apple or from coconut palm toddy, and made nowhere else.

A visit to a working distillery during the cashew season shows the whole process: fruit crushed underfoot in a stone *colmbi*, juice fermented in earthen pots, then triple-distilled in copper pots over wood fire. Tasting at the still, alongside the producer's explanation, is the best possible introduction to a drink that is frequently misunderstood.

---

**SPICE PLANTATION & GOAN LUNCH**

The plantations inland from Ponda grow pepper, nutmeg, cardamom, vanilla and areca under shade, and most run guided walks followed by a traditional Goan meal served on banana leaf.

The lunch is the point as much as the walk, xacuti, sorpotel, fish curry rice, sannas, and bebinca to finish, with the spices identified an hour earlier appearing directly in the dishes. It is the most efficient way to understand how Portuguese, Konkani and Malabar influences combined into Goan cooking.

---

**BASILICA OF BOM JESUS**

Completed in 1605, the Basilica of Bom Jesus is the most important Christian monument in India and part of the Old Goa UNESCO World Heritage inscription.

It holds the body of St. Francis Xavier, the Jesuit missionary who died in 1552, preserved in a silver casket set within an elaborate marble mausoleum designed by a Florentine sculptor. The relics are exposed publicly only once every ten years, drawing enormous crowds. The laterite exterior, deliberately left unplastered, has weathered into a striking dark red.

---

**SE CATHEDRAL**

Se Cathedral is the largest church in Asia, built over eighty years to commemorate a Portuguese victory and completed in 1652 in a Portuguese-Manueline style with a Tuscan exterior.

One of its two original bell towers collapsed; the surviving tower holds the Golden Bell, the largest in Asia and celebrated for its tone. Inside, the main altar is dedicated to St. Catherine and gilded across its full height. The scale of the interior, particularly for a building of this age, is genuinely imposing.

---

**CHURCH OF ST. FRANCIS OF ASSISI**

Built by Franciscan friars in 1661 on the site of an earlier chapel, this church combines a Portuguese-Manueline doorway retained from the original with a later Baroque interior.

The interior is the most decorated in Old Goa, carved and gilded woodwork, painted panels depicting the life of St. Francis, and a floor of inscribed gravestones. The adjoining convent now houses the Archaeological Museum, which holds portraits of the Portuguese viceroys and pre-Portuguese sculpture from across Goa.

---

**PALOLEM BEACH**

Palolem is the best known of the southern beaches, a near-perfect crescent of pale sand about a kilometre long, closed at each end by rocky headlands and backed by coconut palm.

The bay is shallow and calm for most of the season, which makes it good for swimming and kayaking. Development is limited to low-rise huts and shacks set back from the sand, and the atmosphere remains markedly slower than the north. Boat trips run to nearby Butterfly and Honeymoon beaches.

---

**AGONDA BEACH**

A few kilometres north of Palolem, Agonda is longer, wider and quieter, a three-kilometre stretch with very little built along it and a strong local commitment to keeping it that way.

Olive ridley turtles nest at the northern end, and the area is protected during the season. There is no nightlife to speak of and few vendors. For travellers who want the southern Goa coast without any crowd at all, Agonda is generally the recommendation.

---

**CABO DE RAMA FORT**

Cabo de Rama occupies a headland between Agonda and Colva, and is far older than the Portuguese presence. The site was fortified by local rulers long before it changed hands in 1763.

Much of it is now ruined, but the outer walls, a small chapel still in use, and cannon positions remain. The cliffs below drop directly to the sea, and the viewpoint at the far end of the promontory is one of the most dramatic on the Goan coast, with almost no visitors on most days.

---

**DUDHSAGAR FALLS**

Dudhsagar, "sea of milk", falls in four tiers from a height of over three hundred metres on the Mandovi River, on the Goa–Karnataka border within the Bhagwan Mahaveer Sanctuary.

A railway line crosses the face of the falls, and trains passing directly in front of the cascade are among the most photographed images in India. Access is by permitted jeep from Mollem followed by a short walk. The falls are at their most spectacular from July through to early winter.

---

**BIG FOOT MUSEUM, LOUTOLIM**

Big Foot is an open-air recreation of a traditional Goan village, built to preserve rural crafts and daily life as they were before tourism and migration reshaped the state.

Life-size scenes show the potter, the toddy tapper, the farmer, the cobbler, alongside demonstrations of feni distilling and a 400-year-old Goan house. It is unashamedly a reconstruction rather than a preserved site, but it is well made and genuinely informative on how rural Goa functioned.

### Additional Information
Hampi is extensive and largely unshaded, early morning and late afternoon are strongly preferred. Sun protection and water are essential year-round.

---

**VIRUPAKSHA TEMPLE**

Virupaksha is the oldest functioning structure in Hampi and one of the oldest continuously worshipped temples in India, its origins dating back to the seventh century and long predating the Vijayanagara Empire.

Its eastern gopuram rises nine storeys above the bazaar street. The complex includes pillared halls, shrines and a temple elephant that blesses visitors. A small chamber behind the sanctum displays an inverted image of the main tower projected through a pinhole, a demonstration of camera obscura built into the architecture.

---

**VITTALA TEMPLE & STONE CHARIOT**

The Vittala Temple is the highest achievement of Vijayanagara architecture, and its stone chariot, a shrine carved as a processional cart, complete with wheels that once turned, is among the most recognised images in India.

The temple's *maha mandapa* is supported by slender carved pillars that produce distinct musical notes when struck, each column resonating differently. The complex also holds a market street, a tank and elaborately carved halls. It sits some distance from the main site and is reached by electric shuttle or on foot.

---

**HAMPI BAZAAR**

Running east from the Virupaksha gopuram, the Hampi Bazaar is a colonnaded street more than a kilometre long, once one of the great trading places of the medieval world where horses, gemstones and textiles changed hands.

Portuguese and Persian travellers left accounts of its wealth. The stone pavilions survive along both sides, and walking its length with the temple tower behind gives a clear sense of the scale on which Vijayanagara operated at its height.

---

**LOTUS MAHAL & ZENANA ENCLOSURE**

The Lotus Mahal sits within the walled Zenana Enclosure, the women's quarters of the royal centre, and is unusual in Hampi for its blend of Islamic arches with Hindu structural elements.

Its arched openings and lotus-bud shaping give it its name, and its design incorporated water channels for cooling. The surrounding enclosure includes watchtowers and the treasury building. The architecture here is markedly different from the temple complexes and reflects the cosmopolitan character of the Vijayanagara court.

---

**ELEPHANT STABLES**

Adjacent to the Zenana Enclosure, the Elephant Stables comprise eleven domed chambers in a row, each large enough to house a royal elephant, with a central pavilion for musicians and ceremonial staff.

The domes alternate in design, ribbed, fluted, octagonal, and the Islamic influence is unmistakable. The building survived the sack of Vijayanagara in 1565 largely intact, and is among the best-preserved structures on the entire site.

---

**MATANGA HILL SUNRISE**

Matanga is the highest point in Hampi, and the climb to the small temple at its summit is the classic way to begin a day here, a steep twenty to thirty minutes on rock-cut steps in the dark.

From the top, the sun rises over a landscape of granite boulders, banana plantations and the Tungabhadra River, with temple towers emerging from the haze in every direction. It is the single best vantage point for understanding how the ruined city sits within its extraordinary terrain.

---

**HEMAKUTA HILL TEMPLES**

Hemakuta Hill rises immediately behind the Virupaksha Temple and is covered with more than thirty small shrines, many predating the Vijayanagara period and built in a simpler pre-empire style.

The hill is an easy climb and the usual choice for sunset, with views over the temple complex and the boulder fields beyond. Because the shrines are compact and close together, it also offers the clearest picture of how temple architecture in this region developed before the imperial era.

---

**CORACLE RIDE ON THE TUNGABHADRA**

The coracle, a round basket boat of woven bamboo waterproofed with hide or tar, has been used on the Tungabhadra for centuries and remains in daily use.

Rides run from the river bank near Virupaksha, spinning gently downstream past bathing ghats, submerged shrines and boulders the size of houses. It is a short, simple experience and provides a completely different angle on the site, most of Hampi's riverside structures were designed to be seen from the water.

---

**QUEEN'S BATH**

Despite its name, the Queen's Bath was most likely a royal pleasure pavilion, a plain exterior enclosing an arcaded courtyard with projecting balconies overlooking a large sunken bath.

Water was supplied by an aqueduct, and the surrounding corridor is vaulted with ornamental stucco. The contrast between the austere outside and the decorated interior is deliberate, and it illustrates the Indo-Islamic architectural vocabulary that the Vijayanagara rulers adopted for their secular buildings.

## MYSORE

### Highlights

### Additional Information
Photography is not permitted inside the palace, and shoes must be removed. Dasara falls in September or October and books out months ahead.

---

**MYSORE PALACE**

The Amba Vilas Palace is the second most visited monument in India after the Taj Mahal, and among the most flamboyant buildings in the country, an Indo-Saracenic composition of domes, arches and colonnades completed in 1912 after fire destroyed its predecessor.

The interior runs to stained glass from Glasgow, cast-iron columns, Belgian chandeliers, inlaid doors and painted galleries. On Sunday evenings and during Dasara, close to a hundred thousand light bulbs outline the entire building, an effect that has to be seen to be believed.

---

**CHAMUNDI HILL & TEMPLE**

Chamundi Hill rises about a thousand feet above Mysore, and the temple at its summit is dedicated to Chamundeshwari, the presiding deity of the royal family and the goddess said to have slain the demon Mahishasura, from whom the city takes its name.

A monolithic statue of Nandi, carved from a single boulder and more than four metres high, stands partway up the thousand-step path. The summit offers a full view over Mysore and the palace below.

---

**DEVARAJA MARKET**

Devaraja Market has operated for well over a century and remains a working produce market rather than a tourist one: long aisles of flowers, fruit, bananas of a dozen varieties, and pyramids of kumkum powder in intense colour.

The flower section alone justifies the visit, supplying jasmine and marigold to temples across the city. It is dense, loud and photogenic, and a guide is genuinely useful here for navigating and explaining what is being sold and to whom.

---

**ST. PHILOMENA'S CATHEDRAL**

Built between 1933 and 1941 in a Neo-Gothic style modelled on Cologne Cathedral, St. Philomena's is one of the largest churches in India, with twin spires rising over 175 feet.

The stained glass depicts scenes from the life of Christ, and a crypt beneath the main altar holds a relic of St. Philomena. Its scale and European styling are unexpected in a South Indian city, and it reflects the Wodeyar rulers' patronage of communities across faiths.

---

**BRINDAVAN GARDENS**

Laid out below the Krishnarajasagar Dam in the 1930s, Brindavan is a terraced ornamental garden in the Mughal style, with symmetrical parterres, water channels and fountains extending across some sixty acres.

The main attraction is the evening musical fountain show, when the fountains are lit and choreographed to music. The gardens are busiest at weekends. The dam itself, an engineering achievement of its era, is visible above the terraces.

---

**MYSORE SILK WEAVING**

Mysore silk is woven with pure silk and genuine gold zari, and the state-run weaving factory allows visitors to see the full process, from reeling and dyeing to weaving on power and handlooms.

The distinctive weight and lustre of a Mysore silk sari come from the density of the weave and the quality of the zari thread. The factory outlet sells directly, with certification of gold and silk content. It is one of the more genuinely informative industrial visits in South India.

---

**SANDALWOOD OIL FACTORY**

Mysore has been associated with sandalwood for centuries, and the government sandalwood oil factory, established in 1916, still processes heartwood into oil by steam distillation.

The visit covers the chipping, distillation and grading of an oil so valuable it is measured by the gram. Sandalwood harvesting is now tightly regulated to protect the tree, and the factory explains that conservation dimension as well. Soaps, oils and carvings are available at the attached outlet.

---

**DASARA FESTIVAL** *(seasonal)*

Mysore Dasara is the state festival of Karnataka and among the great spectacles of India, ten days culminating in a procession in which a golden howdah carrying the idol of Chamundeshwari is borne through the city on a caparisoned elephant.

The palace is illuminated nightly, and the city fills with music, wrestling and exhibitions. Accommodation across Mysore books out many months in advance and prices rise sharply. For guests able to plan far ahead, it is worth building an entire itinerary around.

---

## COORG

### Coorg Attractions:
- Coffee Estate Walk

### Highlights

### Additional Information
Coorg's roads are winding and the district is spread out, distances take longer than the map suggests. Monsoon here is heavy, though the landscape is at its most beautiful immediately after.

---

**COFFEE ESTATE WALK**

Coorg produces a substantial share of India's coffee, and the estates here grow it in the shade of native trees rather than in open sun, with pepper vines trained up the same trunks.

An estate walk covers the flowering, picking, pulping, fermenting and drying cycle, and finishes with cupping. Many estates are family-owned across several generations and offer stays as well as tours. The shade-grown system also supports notable birdlife, which makes the walk rewarding beyond the crop itself.

---

**ABBEY FALLS**

Abbey Falls drops through a gorge surrounded by coffee estates and spice plantations a short drive from Madikeri, viewed from a hanging bridge built directly opposite the cascade.

The approach passes through private estate land, with coffee and cardamom growing on both sides of the path. The falls are strongest between July and October. The setting, dense green on all sides, water falling into a rock pool, is the classic image of Coorg.

---

**RAJA'S SEAT**

Raja's Seat is a small garden pavilion on a ridge in Madikeri, built as a viewing point for the Kodagu kings, who came here to watch the sun set over the valleys.

The view runs across layered ridgelines that fill with mist in the evening, and the surrounding gardens are planted with seasonal flowers and fountains. It is busiest at sunset, when the entire town seems to gather, and a musical fountain runs in the evening.

---

**DUBARE ELEPHANT CAMP**

Dubare, on the bank of the Cauvery, was historically a training camp for elephants used in the Mysore Dasara procession and now operates as a forest department camp open to visitors.

Under keeper supervision, guests can observe and assist with feeding and bathing. There is no riding or performance. Naturalists explain elephant biology and the camp's role in managing human-elephant conflict, and the riverside forest around it is good for birdwatching.

---

**TALACAUVERY**

Talacauvery is the source of the Cauvery, one of the great rivers of South India, rising on the slopes of Brahmagiri at around 1,300 metres.

A temple tank marks the spring, and pilgrims come to bathe here, particularly during the annual Tula Sankramana when the spring is believed to surge. A flight of steps climbs from the tank to a viewpoint over the surrounding hills, and on clear days the view extends a considerable distance across the Ghats.

---

**NAMDROLING MONASTERY**

At Bylakuppe, one of the largest Tibetan settlements in India, the Namdroling Monastery, widely called the Golden Temple, houses several thousand monks and nuns.

Its prayer hall holds three gilded statues nearly twenty metres tall, set against walls painted in intense colour with scenes from Tibetan Buddhist tradition. Visitors are welcome, and attending an afternoon prayer session, with the sound of horns and massed chanting, is a genuinely striking experience.

---

**NAGARHOLE NATIONAL PARK**

Nagarhole forms part of the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve and connects with Bandipur and Wayanad, creating one of the most important tiger and elephant landscapes in India.

Jeep and boat safaris operate morning and evening. Elephant herds, gaur, dhole, sambar and spotted deer are common; tiger and leopard are present in good numbers though sightings require luck. The Kabini backwater section is particularly known for its concentration of elephants in the dry months.

---

**KODAVA CUISINE EXPERIENCE**

The Kodava community has a distinct martial heritage and a cuisine to match: meat-heavy, sharply spiced, and unlike anything in the surrounding regions.

The signature dish is *pandi curry*, pork slow-cooked with kachampuli, a dark sour vinegar made from a local fruit, served with *kadambuttu* rice dumplings. Wild mushroom, bamboo shoot and river fish appear seasonally. Homestay meals with a Kodava family are by far the best way to encounter it.

---

**MAHABALESHWAR TEMPLE**

The Mahabaleshwar Temple is one of the most significant Shiva shrines on the west coast, built in Dravidian style and holding the Atmalinga, which by legend was carried here and fixed to the ground by trickery.

Pilgrims come from across India, and the town's character is shaped by them rather than by tourism. The temple sits close to the main beach, and the coexistence of serious pilgrimage and a relaxed traveller scene within a few hundred metres is what makes Gokarna distinctive.

---

**OM BEACH**

Om Beach takes its name from its shape, two joined crescents forming the outline of the sacred symbol, best appreciated from the headland above.

It is the most popular of Gokarna's beaches, with a scattering of shacks and huts along the sand but nothing approaching Goa's development. The northern crescent is calm and good for swimming; the southern is quieter. Boat trips run from here to the more isolated beaches further south.

---

**KUDLE BEACH**

Kudle is the closest of the cliff beaches to town, a broad crescent reached by a walking path over the headland from Gokarna, or by a longer road approach.

It is wider and more open than Om, with a line of low-key cafés set back from the sand and a strong sunset. Yoga and meditation classes operate here through the season. The walk over from town takes around twenty minutes and offers the first proper view of the coastline south.

---

**HALF MOON & PARADISE BEACHES**

Beyond Om lie Half Moon and, further still, Paradise, smaller, progressively more remote coves reachable only on foot over the headlands or by boat.

Facilities are minimal by design; Paradise in particular has very little beyond a few shacks. The isolation is the appeal, and the walk between them along cliff paths above the sea is among the finest short coastal walks in India. Boats return to Om for those who prefer not to walk back.

---

**COASTAL BEACH TREK**

The full trek links Gokarna town beach, Kudle, Om, Half Moon and Paradise in a single route along cliff paths, headlands and sand.

It takes three to five hours depending on pace and swimming stops, with sea views for almost the entire length. The path is unshaded in parts and involves some scrambling. Starting early avoids the heat, and arranging a boat pickup at Paradise makes the return straightforward.

---

**MIRJAN FORT**

Some twenty kilometres from Gokarna, Mirjan Fort is a laterite fortification of the sixteenth century, associated with Queen Chennabhairadevi, who ruled this coast for over half a century and was known to the Portuguese as the Pepper Queen.

The fort has high walls, bastions, a moat and wells within the enclosure, and it is very lightly visited. Its scale and the quality of its stonework are unexpected, and it makes a rewarding stop on the drive between Gokarna and the interior.

---

**YANA CAVES**

Yana lies inland in the Sahyadri forest, where two enormous black crystalline limestone outcrops, Bhairaveshwara Shikhara and Mohini Shikhara, rise abruptly from dense evergreen forest.

A cave shrine to Shiva sits at the base of the larger formation, reached by a walk of about a kilometre through forest. The rock is dramatically weathered and unlike anything else in the region. It is a modest detour from the coast and a genuinely unusual landscape.

## MADURAI

### What's Included

### Highlights
- A living temple city of fourteen towers covered in thousands of sculptures

### Additional Information
Meenakshi Temple requires modest dress and shoes to be removed. Cameras and phones are restricted inside, arrangements are made in advance. The temple closes in the afternoon and reopens for evening worship.

---

**MEENAKSHI AMMAN TEMPLE**

Meenakshi Amman is not a monument but a functioning temple city, covering fourteen acres at the centre of Madurai and drawing tens of thousands of worshippers every day.

Fourteen gopurams rise above the complex, the tallest nearly fifty metres, each covered in thousands of brightly painted stucco figures. Inside are the Hall of a Thousand Pillars, no two carved alike, the golden lotus tank, and shrines to Meenakshi and Sundareswarar. Worship here has continued for centuries, that continuity is what makes it extraordinary.

---

**THIRUMALAI NAYAKKAR PALACE**

Built in 1636 by the Nayak ruler whose name it carries, this palace originally covered four times its present area; what survives is the main hall and dance chamber.

The scale remains impressive, massive circular columns some twenty metres high supporting scalloped arches and a domed ceiling, in a style blending Dravidian and Islamic architecture. A sound-and-light show recounting the Silappathikaram epic runs in the evenings.

---

**GANDHI MEMORIAL MUSEUM**

Housed in a seventeenth-century palace belonging to the Nayak queen, the Gandhi Memorial Museum is one of only a handful of such museums in India and traces the independence movement in detail.

Its most affecting exhibit is the blood-stained cloth Gandhi was wearing when he was assassinated. Madurai holds particular significance in his life, it was here in 1921 that he first adopted the loincloth, after seeing the poverty of agricultural labourers in the surrounding countryside.

---

**NIGHT CEREMONY AT MEENAKSHI**

Each evening before closing, an image of Sundareswarar is carried in procession from his shrine to Meenakshi's chamber, accompanied by music, drums and lamps, and returned each morning.

The ceremony has been performed nightly for centuries and is open to visitors. Arriving early to secure a position along the route is worthwhile. Of everything the temple offers, this is the experience that most clearly conveys that it is a living institution rather than a preserved one.

---

**PUTHU MANDAPAM BAZAAR**

Puthu Mandapam is a pillared hall built in the seventeenth century directly outside the temple's eastern gopuram, its columns carved with Nayak rulers and mythological figures.

The hall has been occupied for generations by tailors, booksellers, brassware dealers and craftsmen working between the carved pillars. It is one of the few places where a monument of this age remains in full daily commercial use, and it is the best place in Madurai to have a shirt made in a few hours.

---

**CHETTINAD CUISINE EXPERIENCE**

Chettinad cooking, from the merchant Chettiar community of the surrounding region, is the most complex and heavily spiced cuisine in Tamil Nadu: built on freshly ground masalas using star anise, stone flower, marathi moggu and black pepper.

Signature dishes include chicken Chettinad, meen kuzhambu and a range of dosai and appam variants. A meal at a family-run kitchen, ideally in one of the Chettinad mansion towns, is the way to encounter it properly, and the mansions themselves are extraordinary.

---

**ALAGAR KOVIL**

Some twenty kilometres north of Madurai in the Alagar Hills, this temple is dedicated to Vishnu as Kallalagar, brother of Meenakshi, and is central to Madurai's great Chithirai festival.

During the festival the deity is carried from here into the Vaigai River in an enormous procession watched by hundreds of thousands. Outside festival season the temple is quiet and set in forested hills, with a spring shrine higher up the slope reached by a walking path.

---

## THANJAVUR

### Thanjavur Attractions:
- Brihadeeswarar Temple
- Thanjavur Royal Palace
- Saraswathi Mahal Library
- Art Gallery & Bronze Gallery
- Thanjavur Painting Workshop
- Airavatesvara Temple, Darasuram
- Gangaikonda Cholapuram

### What's Included
- Professional licensed local guide
- Private air-conditioned vehicle with dedicated driver
- Guided interpretation of the Great Living Chola Temples

### Highlights
- A thousand-year-old temple built entirely of granite, in a region with no granite
- A capstone weighing eighty tonnes, raised without modern machinery
- Chola bronzes among the finest metal sculptures ever made

### Additional Information
The three Great Living Chola Temples are spread across a wide area; a full day is needed to see them properly. Stone floors become very hot by midday, early visits are advised.

---

**BRIHADEESWARAR TEMPLE**

Completed in 1010 by Rajaraja Chola I, the Brihadeeswarar Temple is one of the supreme achievements of Indian architecture and the centrepiece of the Great Living Chola Temples UNESCO inscription.

Its vimana rises sixty-six metres, capped by a single stone weighing around eighty tonnes. The entire structure is granite, quarried and transported from a considerable distance, as the region has none. A Nandi carved from a single block guards the entrance, and the frescoes in the inner passage are original Chola work.

---

**THANJAVUR ROYAL PALACE**

The palace complex was built by the Nayaks and later extended by the Marathas, and it remains partly occupied by descendants of the royal family.

Its halls, arsenal tower and bell tower can be climbed for views over the town toward the temple vimana. The architecture is layered rather than unified, reflecting successive rulers, and the complex houses the library, art gallery and bronze collection that make Thanjavur essential for anyone interested in South Indian art.

---

**SARASWATHI MAHAL LIBRARY**

Established by the Nayak rulers and greatly expanded under the Maratha king Serfoji II, the Saraswathi Mahal is among the oldest libraries in Asia still in existence.

It holds tens of thousands of palm-leaf and paper manuscripts in Tamil, Sanskrit, Marathi and Telugu, covering medicine, literature, music and philosophy. A museum section displays selected manuscripts, illustrated volumes and Serfoji's own European acquisitions, which reveal an unusually cosmopolitan scholarly court.

---

**ART GALLERY & BRONZE GALLERY**

Housed within the palace, the art gallery holds an outstanding collection of Chola stone sculpture and bronzes, gathered from temples across the region.

Chola bronzes, cast by the lost-wax method, are regarded as among the finest metal sculptures ever produced anywhere, the Nataraja form in particular. Seeing them at close range, in numbers, is a very different experience from encountering single examples in Western museums.

---

**THANJAVUR PAINTING WORKSHOP**

Thanjavur painting is a classical South Indian form characterised by rich colour, compact composition, inlaid semi-precious stones and gold foil applied over a gesso relief.

A workshop visit shows the full sequence: board preparation, drawing, gesso build-up, gilding and stone setting. The technique has been passed down through families, and watching gold leaf laid onto raised gesso is the clearest possible explanation of why these works have the depth they do.

---

**AIRAVATESVARA TEMPLE, DARASURAM**

The third of the Great Living Chola Temples, Airavatesvara at Darasuram is smaller than Thanjavur but finer in its detail, built by Rajaraja Chola II in the twelfth century.

Its front mandapa is conceived as a chariot drawn by horses, and the base carries miniature friezes of remarkable delicacy. A set of steps produces musical tones when struck. For visitors who find the scale of Brihadeeswarar overwhelming, Darasuram is the more intimate and arguably more beautiful building.

---

**GANGAIKONDA CHOLAPURAM**

Built by Rajendra Chola I to commemorate his military expedition to the Ganges, Gangaikonda Cholapuram was the Chola capital for around 250 years and holds the second of the three Great Living Chola Temples.

Its vimana is slightly lower than Thanjavur's but more curved in profile, and its sculpture is generally considered more refined. The site is quiet and receives far fewer visitors than Thanjavur, which makes it one of the most rewarding stops in the entire Chola circuit.

---

## MAHABALIPURAM

### Mahabalipuram Attractions:
- Shore Temple
- Pancha Rathas
- Arjuna's Penance

- Stone Carving Workshops

### What's Included
- Professional licensed local guide
- Private air-conditioned vehicle with dedicated driver
- UNESCO site entry arrangements

### Highlights
- Temples carved whole from single outcrops of granite
- The largest bas-relief in the world, cut into a rock face
- A stone-carving tradition still practised in the streets around the monuments

### Additional Information
The site is compact and largely walkable. Early morning gives the best light on the Shore Temple and avoids both heat and crowds.

---

**SHORE TEMPLE**

Built in the early eighth century under Narasimhavarman II, the Shore Temple stands directly on the beach, one of the earliest free-standing stone temples in South India and the survivor of a group that once stood along this coast.

Its two towers rise above a compound wall lined with seated Nandi figures, and centuries of salt wind have softened the carving into something closer to sculpture than architecture. It is at its finest at sunrise, with the sea directly behind it.

---

**PANCHA RATHAS**

The Five Rathas are monolithic temples, each carved downward out of a single granite outcrop, named after the Pandava brothers of the Mahabharata.

No two are alike, each demonstrates a different architectural form, and together they effectively catalogue the styles available to seventh-century builders. Several were left unfinished, which makes the carving method legible: the sculptors worked from the top down, and the incomplete examples show exactly how.

---

**ARJUNA'S PENANCE**

Arjuna's Penance is the largest bas-relief in the world: a rock face nearly thirty metres long and fifteen high, covered with more than a hundred figures of gods, humans, and animals.

A natural cleft down the centre once carried water, representing the descent of the Ganges. The life-size elephants at the base are among the finest animal sculptures in Indian art. The whole composition reads as a single narrative and repays a slow, guided examination.

---

**KRISHNA'S BUTTERBALL**

Krishna's Butterball is a granite boulder some six metres high, resting on a smooth slope at an angle that appears impossible, and it has not moved in more than a thousand years.

Attempts were made during the colonial period to pull it down with elephants; it did not shift. It sits within the wider monument complex, and the surrounding smooth rock slopes are a popular gathering place in the late afternoon.

---

**CAVE TEMPLES & MANDAPAS**

Scattered across the hill above the main site are a series of rock-cut cave temples, or mandapas, each cut horizontally into the granite and containing carved panels.

The Varaha Mandapa, Mahishasuramardini Mandapa and Krishna Mandapa hold some of the finest Pallava relief sculpture, including a celebrated panel of Durga in battle. They are less visited than the main monuments and quieter, and the carving is in several cases better preserved.

---

**STONE CARVING WORKSHOPS**

Mahabalipuram's carving tradition never stopped. The streets around the monuments are lined with working sculptors producing temple statuary, commissioned pieces and export work in granite, using largely the same tools as their predecessors.

Visiting a workshop while carvers are working gives an immediate sense of what the ancient monuments required in labour and skill. Many are happy to explain their process, and the government college of sculpture nearby trains the next generation.

---

**MAHABALIPURAM BEACH**

The beach runs along the site's eastern edge, wide and open, with fishing boats drawn up on the sand and the Shore Temple visible along the shoreline.

It is a working beach rather than a swimming one, currents can be strong, but it is excellent for walking, particularly at dawn when the fishing fleet returns. The combination of monument and working coastline in a single view is what gives Mahabalipuram its particular character.

**FRENCH QUARTER WALK**

Pondicherry was French for nearly three hundred years, and the Ville Blanche retains a grid of streets that feels transplanted: mustard-yellow walls, bougainvillea over compound gates, louvred shutters and street signs still lettered in French.

The quarter is separated from the Tamil town by a canal, and the architectural contrast either side of it is striking. Many colonial buildings now house boutique hotels and cafés. It is compact, entirely walkable, and best explored slowly.

---

**SRI AUROBINDO ASHRAM**

Founded in 1926 by the philosopher and independence activist Sri Aurobindo and his collaborator known as the Mother, the ashram sits at the heart of the French Quarter and remains an active spiritual community.

The main building holds the samadhi of both founders, covered daily in flowers, and visitors sit in silence around it. The ashram runs schools, farms and workshops across Pondicherry, and its presence accounts for much of the town's particular atmosphere.

---

**AUROVILLE & MATRIMANDIR**

Founded in 1968 as an experimental township intended to belong to no nation, Auroville houses residents from dozens of countries living under a shared charter.

At its centre stands the Matrimandir, a vast golden sphere containing a meditation chamber lit by a single shaft of sunlight falling on the largest optically perfect glass globe in the world. Access to the inner chamber requires booking in advance; the surrounding gardens and viewing point are open to day visitors.

---

**PROMENADE BEACH**

The Promenade is a mile-long seafront walkway closed to traffic in the evenings, lined with a war memorial, the old lighthouse, a Gandhi statue and the French-era Hôtel de Ville.

It is where Pondicherry gathers after dark. The beach itself is rocky rather than sandy, but the promenade is among the most pleasant urban seafronts in India, and the morning walk before sunrise is a long-standing local ritual.

---

**BASILICA OF THE SACRED HEART OF JESUS**

Built in the early twentieth century in Gothic Revival style, the Basilica is one of the largest churches in South India, with a striking pale exterior and twin spires.

Its interior holds twenty-eight stained glass panels depicting the life of Christ, and the rose window above the altar is particularly fine. The church remains an active parish, and the contrast between its European Gothic form and the tropical street outside is one of Pondicherry's characteristic juxtapositions.

---

**PARADISE BEACH**

Paradise Beach lies south of town at Chunnambar and is reached by a short boat ride through a backwater creek, which is part of the appeal.

The beach itself is a broad stretch of clean golden sand with basic facilities and no development behind it. Because access is controlled by the boat service, it never becomes overwhelming. The creek crossing passes through mangrove and is worth doing slowly.

---

**FRENCH-TAMIL CULINARY EXPERIENCE**

Pondicherry's cuisine is a genuine creole: French technique meeting Tamil ingredients, producing dishes that exist nowhere else, alongside a strong tradition of French bakery.

A guided food experience typically covers a bakery, a Tamil breakfast of dosai and filter coffee, and a creole lunch of dishes such as *poisson assad* or Pondicherry-style prawn curry. The best cooking is often in small family-run kitchens rather than restaurants on the promenade.

### Additional Information
The Nilgiri Mountain Railway books out well in advance, particularly the Mettupalayam–Coonoor section, early reservation is essential. Ooty is cold in the evenings from December to February.

---

**NILGIRI MOUNTAIN RAILWAY**

Opened in 1908 and inscribed by UNESCO as part of the Mountain Railways of India, the Nilgiri Mountain Railway is the only rack railway in the country, climbing from Mettupalayam to Ooty through a gradient no adhesion railway could manage.

The route passes through sixteen tunnels and over 250 bridges, ascending from tropical lowland into montane forest and tea country in under five hours. A steam locomotive still works the steepest section. It is among the great railway journeys of the world.

---

**BOTANICAL GARDENS**

Laid out in 1848 across terraced slopes covering fifty-five acres, the Government Botanical Gardens hold species collected from across the world, including a fossilised tree trunk estimated at twenty million years old.

The Italian-style garden section was built by prisoners captured during Tipu Sultan's wars. The annual flower show in May is a major event in the region. The gardens are among the best-maintained public spaces in South India and easily fill a morning.

---

**OOTY LAKE**

Constructed in 1824 by the collector who effectively founded the hill station, Ooty Lake was originally created as a fishing ground and now serves as the town's principal recreation area.

Pedal and row boats operate on the water, and a small toy train runs along the shore. It is busy and unashamedly a family attraction rather than a quiet one, but the setting, water surrounded by eucalyptus slopes, retains the character that drew the British here.

---

**DODDABETTA PEAK**

At 2,637 metres, Doddabetta is the highest point in the Nilgiris and the third highest in South India, reached by road to a summit viewing platform with a telescope house.

On a clear morning the view extends across the Nilgiri plateau into Kerala and Karnataka. The approach passes through shola forest and tea, and the summit is often above the cloud line early in the day, which is by far the best time to visit.

---

**TEA ESTATES & FACTORY**

Nilgiri tea is grown at higher elevation than most Indian tea and is prized for its brisk, fragrant character, exported widely and used extensively in blending.

Factory visits cover withering, rolling, oxidation and firing, followed by tasting across grades. The estates around Coonoor are generally more attractive and less crowded than those immediately around Ooty, and several offer walks through the gardens as well as the factory floor.

---

**ROSE GARDEN**

The Government Rose Garden holds one of the largest collections of roses in India, several thousand varieties across terraced beds on a hillside overlooking the town.

Varieties include hybrid teas, floribunda, ramblers and rare black and green roses. The garden is at its peak between May and June. Its position on a slope means the whole collection is visible in layers, and the views over Ooty from the upper terraces are excellent.

---

**COONOOR & SIM'S PARK**

Coonoor, lower and quieter than Ooty, is generally the more appealing base: smaller, greener, and surrounded by the best of the Nilgiri tea estates.

Sim's Park is a botanical garden laid out in a natural bowl, retaining much of the original shola vegetation alongside collected species. Nearby, Lamb's Rock and Dolphin's Nose offer dramatic views over the Coimbatore plains. For travellers wanting hill station character without the crowds, Coonoor is the recommendation.

## LAKSHADWEEP

### Lakshadweep Attractions:
- Agatti Island & Lagoon
- Bangaram Island
- Kadmat Island
- Kavaratti
- Minicoy & Lighthouse
- Scuba Diving & Snorkelling
- Kayaking and Glass-Bottom Boats

### What's Included
- Entry permit processing and documentation
- Inter-island transfers and accommodation
- Licensed diving and watersports operators

### Highlights
- Coral lagoons among the clearest water anywhere in India
- Six islands open to visitors out of thirty-six
- A distinct island culture with its own language on Minicoy

### Additional Information
**Entry permits are mandatory for all non-islanders, including Indian citizens.** Indian travellers no longer require a local sponsor or police clearance certificate. Foreign nationals require a separate special permit in addition to an Indian visa, processed through a registered operator and typically taking four to eight weeks. Peak season runs October to March; services reduce sharply during the June–September monsoon. **Plan a minimum of 60–90 days ahead for international guests.**

---

**AGATTI ISLAND & LAGOON**

Agatti is the gateway to Lakshadweep and the only island with an airstrip, served by flights from Kochi. The runway runs almost the full length of the island, and the approach over the lagoon is spectacular in itself.

The lagoon around Agatti is broad and shallow, shading from white to turquoise to deep blue, and is among the finest in the archipelago. Most itineraries begin and end here, and the island supports a small number of resorts alongside a working fishing community.

---

**BANGARAM ISLAND**

Bangaram is uninhabited except for its single resort, and is one of the few islands open to foreign nationals without additional restriction.

It is a teardrop of sand and coconut palm ringed by a shallow lagoon, with no permanent village and no commercial development beyond the resort. Sea turtles nest on its beaches, and the reef immediately offshore is in excellent condition. It is the most genuinely exclusive destination in Indian waters.

---

**KADMAT ISLAND**

Kadmat is long and narrow, with lagoons on both sides, and is regarded as the best diving base in Lakshadweep, its dive centre is among the longest-established in the islands.

Reef walls, coral gardens and consistently high visibility make it suitable for both certified divers and beginners taking their first open-water course. The island also supports kayaking and windsurfing, and the accommodation is deliberately simple rather than luxurious.

---

**KAVARATTI**

Kavaratti is the administrative capital and the most developed island for visitors, with a marine aquarium, a museum and the Ujra Mosque, noted for its ornately carved ceiling made from driftwood.

Its lagoon supports snorkelling, kayaking and glass-bottom boat trips. Because it holds the territory's administration, it also has the widest range of services in the islands and is generally the most affordable base.

---

**MINICOY & LIGHTHOUSE**

Minicoy is the southernmost inhabited island and culturally distinct from the rest of the archipelago: its people speak Mahl, related to Dhivehi, and its social structure is matrilineal.

The island is known for its traditional dance, its tuna fishing and boat-building, and its lighthouse, built by the British in 1885 and climbable for a full view over the lagoon. The village is organised into distinct quarters, each with its own communal house.

---

**SCUBA DIVING & SNORKELLING**

Lakshadweep's reefs are among the least damaged in the Indian Ocean, with visibility frequently exceeding thirty metres and coral cover in good condition across much of the archipelago.

Dive sites include reef walls, drop-offs and coral gardens supporting turtle, reef shark, napoleon wrasse and large schooling fish. Certified courses run at Kadmat and Agatti. Because visitor numbers are strictly limited by permit, sites are rarely crowded, a significant contrast with more accessible reef destinations.

---

**KAYAKING AND GLASS-BOTTOM BOATS**

The shallow lagoons are ideally suited to kayaking, with calm, clear water and no current across most of the protected areas.

Glass-bottom boat trips offer reef viewing without entering the water, which suits families and non-swimmers, and the clarity means coral and fish are visible in real detail. Both are available on most inhabited islands and are usually arranged directly through the resort or the island administration.

---

## ANDAMAN ISLANDS

### Andaman Attractions:
- Cellular Jail, Port Blair
- Radhanagar Beach, Swaraj Dweep
- Neil Island (Shaheed Dweep)
- Ross Island
- Scuba Diving at Havelock
- Baratang Limestone Caves
- Elephant Beach
- North Bay & Sea Walking

### What's Included
- Inter-island ferry bookings and transfers
- Licensed diving and watersports operators
- Permit and documentation support where applicable

### Highlights
- A colonial prison that became a national memorial
- One of Asia's most highly rated beaches
- Reef diving comparable to Southeast Asia, far less crowded

### Additional Information
Andaman is reached by air via Chennai, Kolkata or Bengaluru, **not directly from Kochi**. Ferries between islands are weather-dependent and book out in peak season. Monsoon runs June to September with reduced services. Tribal reserve areas are strictly protected and closed to visitors.

---

**CELLULAR JAIL, PORT BLAIR**

Completed in 1906, the Cellular Jail was built by the British to hold political prisoners of the Indian independence movement in total isolation, a seven-winged structure radiating from a central tower, with every cell facing the back of another.

Three wings survive, along with the gallows and the solitary cells. The evening sound-and-light show recounts the prison's history. It is a sombre and important site, and it gives the Andamans a historical weight that goes well beyond their reputation as a beach destination.

---

**RADHANAGAR BEACH, SWARAJ DWEEP**

Radhanagar on Swaraj Dweep, formerly Havelock, is regularly ranked among the finest beaches in Asia: a two-kilometre curve of white sand backed by dense forest, with no development on the beach itself.

The water is calm and shallow well out from shore, and the sunset here is exceptional. Because building is set back behind the treeline, the beach retains an undeveloped character rare at this level of recognition.

---

**NEIL ISLAND (SHAHEED DWEEP)**

Neil is smaller, flatter and slower than Havelock, with a farming economy alongside tourism and distances short enough to cover by bicycle.

Its beaches are named simply, Bharatpur, Laxmanpur, Sitapur, and Laxmanpur is known for the natural rock bridge exposed at low tide. Snorkelling directly from the shore is possible at several points. For travellers wanting the Andamans without any crowd, Neil is the choice.

---

**ROSS ISLAND**

Ross Island served as the administrative headquarters of the British in the Andamans until an earthquake in 1941, and was known as the Paris of the East for the comfort of its colonial establishment.

It is now abandoned, and the forest has taken the buildings: a church, ballroom, bakery and cemetery held in the grip of enormous banyan roots. Deer and peacocks roam freely. The combination of ruin and reclamation is genuinely striking, and the crossing from Port Blair takes twenty minutes.

---

**SCUBA DIVING AT HAVELOCK**

Havelock is the principal diving base in the Andamans, with well-established centres offering everything from introductory dives to full certification.

Sites include coral gardens, wrecks and drop-offs supporting manta, reef shark, turtle and large schooling fish, with visibility frequently excellent. The reefs are less trafficked than comparable destinations in Thailand or Indonesia, and the standard of instruction at the established centres is high.

---

**BARATANG LIMESTONE CAVES**

Reached by a road through reserve forest followed by a boat ride through mangrove creek, the Baratang limestone caves are formations of stalactite and stalagmite built up over millennia.

The journey is a substantial part of the experience, the mangrove creek in particular is dense and atmospheric. The road passes through a protected tribal reserve where stopping and photography are strictly prohibited, and travel is by escorted convoy at fixed times.

---

**ELEPHANT BEACH**

Elephant Beach on Havelock is reached by boat or by a forty-minute forest trek, and is the main centre for watersports on the island.

Snorkelling is possible directly from the shore, with coral close in, and the beach offers jet skiing, banana boat rides and sea walking. It is busier than Radhanagar and more activity-focused; the forest walk in is worthwhile for those who prefer to arrive on foot.

---

**NORTH BAY & SEA WALKING**

North Bay, close to Port Blair, is the most accessible reef in the Andamans and the usual place for first-time snorkellers and sea walkers.

Sea walking involves descending to the seabed wearing a weighted helmet supplied with air from the surface, allowing non-swimmers to walk on the reef floor among fish and coral. Glass-bottom boats and semi-submarines also operate here. It works well as a day trip from Port Blair.

---

## SRI LANKA

### Sri Lanka Attractions:
- Sigiriya Rock Fortress
- Temple of the Sacred Tooth Relic, Kandy
- Galle Fort
- Ella & the Kandy–Ella Train
- Yala National Park
- Nuwara Eliya Tea Country
- Anuradhapura & Polonnaruwa
- Mirissa Whale Watching
- Arugam Bay

### What's Included
- Private vehicle with English-speaking chauffeur-guide
- Accommodation and internal transfers
- National park permits and safari arrangements

### Highlights
- A palace built on the summit of a two-hundred-metre rock in the fifth century
- One of the world's most photographed train journeys
- The highest leopard density of any national park on earth

### Additional Information
**Citizens of forty countries, including the UK, US, Germany, France and India, are eligible for a free 30-day tourist ETA, though the ETA must still be obtained before arrival.** Sri Lanka has two monsoons: the south and west coasts are best December to April, the east coast May to September. **March is the strongest single month for island-wide travel.**

---

**SIGIRIYA ROCK FORTRESS**

Sigiriya rises two hundred metres from the surrounding plain, and on its summit are the remains of a royal citadel built in the fifth century by King Kasyapa, palace foundations, cisterns and gardens laid out on the rock itself.

The ascent passes the mirror wall, inscribed with graffiti more than a thousand years old, and a gallery of frescoes painted on a sheer rock face. The approach through symmetrical water gardens is among the oldest landscaped designs in the world. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site and Sri Lanka's defining monument.

---

**TEMPLE OF THE SACRED TOOTH RELIC, KANDY**

The Sri Dalada Maligawa in Kandy houses a tooth relic of the Buddha, one of the most venerated objects in the Buddhist world and historically a symbol of sovereignty over the island.

The relic is kept in a gold casket within an inner chamber, revealed only during ceremonies. Daily *puja* with drumming and offerings takes place three times a day and is open to visitors. The annual Esala Perahera procession, with caparisoned elephants and dancers, is among Asia's great festivals.

---

**GALLE FORT**

Built by the Portuguese and greatly extended by the Dutch in the seventeenth century, Galle Fort is the largest remaining European-built fortification in Asia and a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Its ramparts enclose a living town of colonial houses, churches, mosques and a lighthouse, with streets still following the Dutch grid. Walking the walls at sunset, with the sea on one side and the town on the other, is the essential Galle experience. Many of the old merchant houses are now hotels and galleries.

---

**ELLA & THE KANDY–ELLA TRAIN**

The rail journey from Kandy through the hill country to Ella is among the most celebrated train rides in the world: several hours climbing through tea estates, waterfalls, forest and cloud, with open doorways and windows throughout.

Ella itself is a small town surrounded by walking country: Little Adam's Peak, Ella Rock, and the Nine Arch Bridge, a colonial-era stone viaduct crossing a jungle gorge. Tickets for the reserved observation carriages must be booked well in advance.

---

**YALA NATIONAL PARK**

Yala holds one of the highest concentrations of leopard anywhere in the world, and is the most reliable place in Asia to see the species.

The park's mix of scrub, lagoon and open plain also supports elephant, sloth bear, crocodile, buffalo and a rich birdlife. Safaris run at dawn and late afternoon in open jeeps. Block One is the most productive for leopard but also the busiest; a good guide and an early start make a substantial difference.

---

**NUWARA ELIYA TEA COUNTRY**

Nuwara Eliya sits at nearly 1,900 metres and was built by the British as a hill retreat: half-timbered houses, a golf course, a racecourse and a climate that still surprises visitors arriving from the coast.

The surrounding hills produce the highest-grown Ceylon tea, and estate tours cover plucking, processing and tasting. Horton Plains National Park, with its World's End escarpment dropping almost nine hundred metres, is within reach for an early-morning excursion.

---

**ANURADHAPURA & POLONNARUWA**

Anuradhapura was the island's capital for more than a thousand years, and its ruins cover an enormous area: colossal brick stupas, monastic complexes, and the Sri Maha Bodhi, grown from a cutting of the tree under which the Buddha attained enlightenment and among the oldest documented trees on earth.

Polonnaruwa, the later capital, is more compact and better preserved, with the Gal Vihara rock-cut Buddha figures among the finest sculpture in Sri Lanka. Both are UNESCO World Heritage sites and form the core of the Cultural Triangle.

---

**MIRISSA WHALE WATCHING**

The continental shelf drops sharply close to shore off Mirissa, bringing blue whales, the largest animals ever to have lived, within a few hours' sailing of the harbour.

Sperm whale, fin whale and large pods of spinner dolphin are also seen regularly. The season runs roughly November to April on this coast. Boat operators vary considerably in their standards; we work only with those following responsible approach distances and marine guidelines.

---

**ARUGAM BAY**

On the east coast, Arugam Bay is one of Asia's best-known surf destinations, with a long right-hand point break that works consistently from May through September.

Its season is the exact inverse of the south and west coasts, which makes it the answer for travellers visiting during the Yala monsoon. The village is small and relaxed, and nearby Kumana National Park offers excellent birdwatching as a counterpoint to the surf.
